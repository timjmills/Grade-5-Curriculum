READING

Guided Reading:

Brainpop - Point of View · Mentor Text List for Point of View

Warm-Up: Write two sentences on the board: "I walked to the store." / "She walked to the store." Ask students: "What do you notice about these sentences? How are they different?"

Mini-Lesson (5 minutes): Introduce the concept of Point of View. Define First-Person Point of View: the narrator is part of the story and uses words like "I," "me," and "we." Define Third-Person Point of View: the narrator is not part of the story and uses "he," "she," or "they." Give examples of each type (e.g., First-Person: "I love to read." Third-Person: "She loves to read.").

Activity (5 minutes): Hand out a short paragraph in first-person and third-person points of view. Have students highlight or underline the words that give away the point of view (e.g., "I" or "she").

Wrap-up (2 minutes): Ask students to explain the difference between first and third person to a partner.

Guided Reading:

Warm-Up: Review first-person point of view. Ask a few students to give an example of a first-person sentence.

Mini-Lesson (5 minutes): Read a short passage from a first-person narrator (e.g., a short excerpt from a children’s book like Diary of a Wimpy Kid or The One and Only Ivan, or any book from the mentor text list below). Discuss how the story feels more personal because we are inside the character’s head.

Activity (5 minutes): Have students write a 3-4 sentence story from the first-person perspective. Start with a prompt like: "I was walking through the park when..." After writing, students can share their stories with a partner.

Wrap-up (2 minutes): Ask students: "How does it feel to write from your character's perspective? How do you know it's first person?"

Rotations: See list from Sunday or the differentiation section below.

Guided Reading:

Warm-up (3 minutes): Review third-person point of view. Ask students to give an example of a third-person sentence.

Mini-Lesson (5 minutes): Read a short passage written in third-person from a book like Charlotte's Web or Harry Potter, or any book from the mentor text list below. Discuss how the story feels more like an observer watching the characters. Talk about how we can know different characters' feelings through third-person narration.

Activity (5 minutes): Have students rewrite their Day 2 story from a third-person perspective. Start with a prompt like: "She was walking through the park when..." After writing, students can share their stories with a partner.

Wrap-up (2 minutes): Ask students: "What did you notice when you changed your story to third-person? How do you know it's third person?"

Rotations: See list from Sunday or the differentiation section below.

Guided Reading:

Warm-up (3 minutes): Ask students to recall the two points of view they’ve learned about. Write "First Person" and "Third Person" on the board.

Mini-Lesson (5 minutes): Show a simple story told from both perspectives - example, or any book from the mentor text list below. First Person: "I found a treasure chest." Third Person: "She found a treasure chest." Ask students how the two perspectives feel different.

Activity (5 minutes): In pairs, students take turns retelling a story they know (fairy tale, personal story, etc.) in first and third person. One student tells the story in first person, and the other retells the same story in third person. Afterward, discuss how changing the point of view affects the story.

Wrap-up (2 minutes): Ask: "Which point of view do you like writing from? Why?"

Mentor Text for Point of View:

First-Person Point of View

Diary of a Worm by Doreen Cronin - a humorous look at life from a worm's perspective, giving insight into first-person narration.

I Am Human by Susan Verde - this personal narrative helps students understand emotions and experiences told from a first-person perspective.

The Pain and the Great One by Judy Blume - alternates between a brother and sister’s first-person perspectives, allowing readers to see each character’s point of view.

Henry's Freedom Box by Ellen Levine - a touching first-person account of a boy escaping slavery, rich with emotional depth.

The One and Only Ivan by Katherine Applegate - Ivan, the gorilla, narrates his own story, offering a deeply reflective first-person narrative.

Third-Person Point of View

The Day the Crayons Quit by Drew Daywalt - though each crayon shares its own story, the narrator remains in third-person.

Charlotte’s Web by E.B. White - a timeless third-person omniscient narration, where readers can see into the thoughts and feelings of multiple characters.

Click, Clack, Moo: Cows That Type by Doreen Cronin - a third-person narrative that humorously explores the communication between cows and their farmer.

Because of Winn-Dixie by Kate DiCamillo - a third-person narrative that focuses on the emotional journey of the main character, Opal.

How to Train Your Dragon by Cressida Cowell - third-person narration following the adventures of Hiccup.

Books with Multiple Perspectives

Voices in the Park by Anthony Browne - a story told from four different points of view.

Two Bad Ants by Chris Van Allsburg - alternates between the ants’ perspective and the narrator’s point of view.

Hey, Little Ant by Phillip Hoose and Hannah Hoose - a conversation between a boy and an ant, offering two contrasting perspectives on the same situation.

Adaptation: For students who need more support, provide them with word lists to practice with before they start creating their sentences.

Teachers should choose from the variety of above activities to best suit students abilities.

Objective

Students will be able to organize a data set using tally marks and frequency tables, and create a line plot to display measurement data in fractions of a unit.

Students will be able to draw a scaled pictograph and a scaled bar graph to represent a data set, and solve one- and two-step problems using the graphs.

Students will be able to interpret line graphs to analyze change over time and understand how a circle graph (pie chart) represents parts of a whole (fractions/percentages).

Students will be able to analyze and compare data presented in various graph formats to solve multi-step, real-world problems and make informed decisions.

Students will demonstrate mastery in reading, creating, and interpreting data from various graphs (line plots, bar graphs, line graphs).

Differentiation

Each lesson includes partner practice, common misconceptions independent practice (e.g., plotting fractions), and a challenge to find the mean, median, or mode from the line plot.

Each lesson includes partner practice, common misconceptions independent practice (e.g., importance of a key/scale), and a challenge to create a graph with a multi-unit scale (e.g., each symbol = 5).

Each lesson includes partner practice, common misconceptions independent practice (e.g., reading x- and y-axis), and a challenge to create a simple circle graph from data with easy fractions (e.g., 1/2, 1/4).

Each lesson includes partner practice, common misconceptions independent practice (e.g., comparing two data sets), and a challenge to write their own real-world problems based on a given graph.

Assessment provides options for students (e.g., create a graph vs. interpret a graph). Challenge questions require multi-step analysis or comparing different data representations.
