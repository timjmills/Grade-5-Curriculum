Curriculum Map 2026-2027 · Qatar Foundation School AY calendar · 35 teaching weeks

1. Curriculum Overview

Explorers is the Grade 5 content block — science, social studies and IPC — for 2026-2027. The year is organised into seven themed units across 35 teaching weeks, each unit driven by a hands-on mission that ends in a public exit task.

Weekly rhythm: the unit opens with an Entry Point on the Sunday the unit starts (Day 1). Teaching days are Monday (Hook + Investigate), Tuesday (Apply + Record) and Wednesday (Review + Check). Thursday is review / catch-up — no new learning. The unit closes with a public Exit Task on the last Wednesday.

Every unit carries the same support layers: resources on the unit Padlet, 2–3 links per lesson, 3+ Epic books (Gr 2–4), a Grade 2–3 Readings Pack (Units 3–7), weekly IXL practice (Low / High plus Gr 4–5 stretch) and three-tier ELL/SPED differentiation on every objective. Each unit has a summative assessment (student copy + teacher key) and an exit-task rubric.

What is Matter? Will It Freeze?

IXL this week:

Low: Classify Matter — Solid, Liquid, Gas (Gr 2) · High: Heating, Cooling & Changes of State (Gr 3)

Stretch: Change-of-State Diagrams (Gr 4) · Change-of-State: Melt, Freeze, Evaporate, Condense (Gr 5)

Entry Point (Day 1 — Sunday):

Entry (Sun · Day 1): HBKU / Texas A&M science visit — spark curiosity about matter.

Resources: Teacher Guide (docx) · Student Observation Log (docx)

Mon · Hook + Investigate: What is Matter?

1. Hook — Watch ‘States of Matter’ — STEM & Beyond in short sections. Introduce and revisit key vocabulary: solid, liquid, gas, particle.

2. Entry task — Collect classroom items and ask students to sort the materials in any way they choose.

3. Discuss — Ask: How did you sort the materials? Is there another way to sort them? Can they be sorted as solids, liquids, and gases?

4. Investigate — Sort 20 classroom items by state of matter. Keep the completed sort for reference later in the unit.

Objective: Identify materials and classify them as solids, liquids, or gases.

Assessment: Name classroom materials and explain which state of matter each one belongs to.

Differentiation: Initial syllable / initial-sound support / independent identification and classification.

5. Preview — Introduce the question for tomorrow’s ‘Will it freeze?’ investigation: Do all liquids freeze in the same way? Briefly show the materials: water, hand sanitizer, and 7Up/lemon drink.

6. Resource activity — Watch Crash Course Kids: What’s Matter? (Epic) and complete the Seesaw States of Matter Sort together as a class.

Objective: Name the three main states of matter.

Assessment: Answer: What is the state of matter for this material?

Differentiation: Name one state with support / name two states with some support / name and classify states independently.

Resources — Reading Comprehension: States of Matter

Tue · Start Experiment + Build Understanding: Will It Freeze?

1. Review — Revisit the three states of matter and ask students to recall one property of a solid, liquid, and gas.

2. Video input — Watch a short section of Master The Art of Glass Blowing with Siemon & Salazar | Showcase Series to connect heating, cooling, and changing materials.

3. Start experiment: Will it freeze? — Set up three labelled samples: water, hand sanitizer, and 7Up/lemon drink. Students predict which liquid will freeze fully, partly freeze, or stay liquid, then record their prediction and reason using the science-fair project template. Place all three samples in the freezer to observe during the next lesson.

4. Guided input — Use States of Matter – How do you know? slides to connect observations to properties such as shape, volume, flow, and particle movement.

5. Reading link — Read or refer to Matter: First Science to reinforce vocabulary and examples of solids, liquids, and gases.

Epic read (Gr 2-4, new): Do You Really Want to Skate on Thin Ice? A Book about States of Matter

6. Quick check — Show students one material or image and ask them to name its state of matter and justify their answer using one property.

Objective: Use properties of matter to classify materials and make a prediction about whether different liquids will freeze.

Assessment: Students record a prediction with a reason and answer: What property helped you identify the state of matter?

Differentiation: Name the state with support / give one property-based reason with some support / predict and justify independently.

Wed · Observe + Explain: Will It Freeze?

1. Reveal results — Take out the three labelled samples from Tuesday’s ‘Will it freeze?’ investigation: water, hand sanitizer, and 7Up/lemon drink. Keep the prediction chart visible so students can compare outcomes with their original ideas.

2. Observe and record — Students record whether each liquid fully froze, partly froze/slushed, or stayed liquid. Encourage precise property words such as hard, runny, sticky, solid, liquid, shape, and flow.

3. Compare predictions — Ask: Which result matched your prediction? Which result surprised you? Which liquid froze the most? Which froze the least?

4. Discuss properties — Ask: What changed? What stayed the same? Which observable properties helped us identify each state of matter?

5. Explain the science — Explain that liquids can freeze, but each liquid has its own freezing point. Water usually freezes easily, while mixtures such as hand sanitizer and 7Up/lemon drink may freeze differently because they contain other substances.

6. Record conclusion — Students add a short conclusion to the science-fair project template: “The liquid that froze the most was ___ because ___.”

7. Science fair connection — Students identify how the same investigation process will support their upcoming science fair project: ask a testable question, make a prediction, record evidence, compare results, and write a conclusion using scientific reasoning.

Objective: Tell the state of matter based on observed properties and explain that different liquids have different freezing points.

Assessment: Record the result for each sample and answer: What properties show its state of matter? Did the result match your prediction?

Differentiation: Name the state with support / describe one property with some support / compare results and explain differences independently.

Physical & Chemical Changes

Mon · Review + Model: States of Matter

1. Review — Revisit Monday’s classroom sort and key vocabulary: solid, liquid, gas, particle.

2. Video input — Watch BrainPOP Jr: Solids, Liquids, and Gases in short sections. Pause to check examples of each state.

3. Vocabulary movement game — Brainstorm movements for each state: solid = stand completely still; liquid = move slowly and “flow”; gas = move quickly in every direction without getting close to others. Teacher calls out a state and students model it; extend by letting students lead.

4. Guided sort — Complete the SeeSaw Lesson on Matter together, asking students to justify each choice using properties.

5. Extend — Use Epic: ‘Many Kinds of Matter’ as an additional reading/resource to reinforce examples and vocabulary — open in Epic.

Objective: Model each state of matter through movement and connect it to particle behaviour.

Assessment: Show the movement for a state of matter and explain what the particles are doing.

Differentiation: Model one state with support / model two states with some support / model and explain particle movement independently.

Tue · Start Experiment + Build Understanding: Physical Changes

1. Hook — Watch ‘Physical & Chemical Changes’ (~4 min). Vocab: physical change, melt, reversible, temperature.

2. Teach — This week we transform materials; today we look at physical changes.

3. Melting race — Compare ice, chocolate and butter (scale, 3 beakers, timer, electric skillet). Predict which melts fastest; place each in a beaker on the skillet; note the temperature (water boils at 100°C); measure the melt time; graph the results.

4. Discuss — Are the changes permanent? How could you turn each back into a solid?

5. Quick check — Show one change; is it physical or chemical, and how do you know?

Objective: Say whether a change is physical or chemical.

Assessment: What change has happened?

Differentiation: Say the words physical / chemical · identify some of the time · correctly identify independently.

Resources — Examples of Physical & Chemical Changes · Is it a Physical or Chemical Change? (book) · More examples (Padlet) · Seesaw: Physical & Chemical Changes in Matter · IXL: Identify physical and chemical changes

Wed · Observe + Explain: Physical vs Chemical Changes

1. Review — Sort events physical vs. chemical using the matter games; quick check.

2. Watch — Watch Chemical Changes: Crash Course Kids #19.2; discuss what tells us a chemical change has happened.

3. Experiment — Chemical change: baking soda + vinegar makes a new gas. Record one physical and one chemical change on SeeSaw (Three States of Matter · Chemical or Physical – High).

Objective: State what makes up a chemical change.

Assessment: What things let us know a chemical change has taken place?

Differentiation: Name 1 / 2 / more than 2 indicators.

4. Discuss — Once the gas from the baking soda + vinegar reaction escapes, can we get it back? This change is irreversible — unlike Tuesday’s melting, which was reversible. (Thursday’s investigation will dig deeper into gases and carbonated liquids.)

5. Literacy link (writing) — Revisit the ‘cooked in a mug’ activity; write about how the ingredients transformed.

Objective: Can identify changes that are reversible or irreversible.

Assessment: What change has happened? Is it reversible or irreversible?

Differentiation: Can say the words / identify some of the time / correctly identify reversible and irreversible changes.

Resources — Ice, Butter, Chocolate (Padlet) · States of Matter Millionaire Game

Mon · Investigate + Practice a Fair Test: Thermal Insulators

1. Review + connect — Recall yesterday's chemical-change work (baking soda + vinegar; reversible vs. irreversible changes). Explain that today's investigation tests a different material property — how well a material holds in heat — using the same fair-test process.

2. Hook — Watch Conductors and Insulators, pausing to ask: Why do we use metal pots and pans? What happens if we leave a metal spoon in a hot pot?

3. Teach — Explain that "thermal" means gaining or losing heat; introduce insulator vs. conductor.

4. Investigate — Pour hot water into paper, styrofoam, plastic, and glass cups. Predict, then check: what happens, how does each feel?

5. Fair-test connection — Point out this is a fair test: only the cup material changes; the water amount and time stay the same. Record on the Science Fair template.

Objective: 5-PS1-2: Measure and compare quantities to provide evidence that materials conduct or insulate heat differently.

Assessment: What lets us know a material is a good insulator?

Differentiation: Can say what makes an insulator with support / name 2 insulators with some support / name more than 2 independently.

Tue · Model the Fair Test: Carbonated Liquids

1. Review — Revisit yesterday's cup results; recall the fair-test rule it modeled: change one thing, keep everything else the same.

2. Connect — Look at two bottles of water — sparkling and still. Taste and describe (small amounts, still first, then sparkly); introduce the word carbonated (forced carbonation vs. fermentation). Ask: how could we fair-test what's really in a fizzy drink?

3. Investigate — Run the carbonated-liquids fair test as a class: mix lemon juice + baking soda, then baking soda + vinegar, then baking soda + water. Only the liquid changes each time — the amount of baking soda, container, and time all stay the same.

4. Hypothesis — Write the hypothesis in IF…THEN…BECAUSE form: “If baking soda is added to lemon juice, vinegar, and water, then the lemon juice and vinegar will not act the same as water, because they are acidic and will react with the baking soda (a base), while water (neutral) will show little or no reaction.”

5. Discuss — Once the gas escapes, can we get it back? This change is irreversible.

Objective: Can carry out a fair test and write a hypothesis in IF…THEN…BECAUSE form.

Assessment: Write a hypothesis for the carbonated-liquids investigation using the sentence frame.

Differentiation: Complete the frame with support / write with a word bank / write independently.

Resources — Vinegar & Bicarbonate of Soda Investigation Pack (Twinkl — replaces broken PDF)

Wed · Practice Write-Up: Using the Science Fair Template

1. Review — Look back at Tuesday's carbonated-liquids investigation and hypothesis.

2. Guided write-up — As a class, complete a full write-up of the carbonated-liquids investigation on the Science Fair template: question, hypothesis, materials, procedure, results (as a simple graph), and conclusion.

3. Discuss — What makes a strong conclusion? Model: "My hypothesis was ___ because ___. The data showed ___." Was the reaction with water different from the reaction with vinegar and lemon juice? Why?

4. Preview — Explain that starting Monday, each student chooses their own investigation question for the Science Fair.

Objective: Can complete every section of the Science Fair template using a shared class investigation as a model.

Assessment: Locate and correctly complete each section of the template (question, hypothesis, materials, procedure, results, conclusion).

Differentiation: Complete sections with sentence starters / with a partner / independently.
