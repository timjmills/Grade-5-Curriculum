Grade 5 — Writing

Curriculum Map 2026-2027 · Qatar Foundation School AY calendar · 35 teaching weeks

1. Curriculum Overview

Writing runs Monday and Wednesday inside the literacy block (Grammar takes Sunday and Tuesday; Thursday is catch-up). Six genre units cover the year; weeks 32–35 are review, publishing celebration and portfolio work per the weekly plans.

Every lesson uses the same ≈45-minute routine: quick written review (a TWR 2.0 sentence activity in unit content) → 8–10 min mini-lesson while the class model grows one step → 'say it before you write it' oral rehearsal → ~15 min independent writing on ONE organizer box → 2-minute share and rubric self-check.

Scaffolds for ELL/SPED in every unit: one touchstone text, one growing teacher model, word banks, the organizer IS the draft, and tiered support on the ladder SAY it → COPY the class sentence → COMPLETE a frame → CREATE your own. Low always has a frame and a word bank; dialogue and figurative language are stretch goals. Templates and per-unit stem banks live in the TWR 2.0 Resource Pack; each unit has a co-built rubric and a published exit task.

Grade 5 Writing — Unit 1: Personal Narratives

Unit resources

Unit 1 Writing Padlet — Personal Narratives open Padlet (export)

Lesson routine & scaffolds (every lesson)

Every lesson runs the same way (≈45 min): quick review → 8–10 min mini-lesson while the class model grows one step → “say it before you write it” oral rehearsal → ~15 min independent writing on ONE organizer box → 2-min share + rubric self-check.

Scaffolds for ELL / SPED: one touchstone text + one growing teacher model; word banks (transition words, feeling words, five-senses words); the organizer IS the draft; tiered support (half-written model / sentence frames / open). Dialogue is a stretch goal.

TWR 2.0 upgrade: the quick review is now a 3–5 min WRITTEN sentence activity in unit content (expand a kernel sentence, because/but/so, or combine two short sentences); planning happens in key-word dot jots (2–3 words, never full sentences) so ideas come before sentence-building; and every Wednesday closes with one summary sentence. Every move runs on the same ladder for low-language learners: SAY it → COPY the class sentence → COMPLETE a frame → CREATE your own; Low always has a frame and a word bank. Each lesson below carries a “TWR moves” line; templates and per-unit stem banks are in the TWR 2.0 Resource Pack.

Unit exit task

Student-friendly task: Write a true story about ONE small moment from your life — about half a page to one page. Tell it in order (beginning, middle, end), use describing words and your feelings, and write it in the past tense. Adding a line of dialogue is a stretch goal. Then fix it up and publish it.

How the lessons build to it:

Wk 1 · L1 (Mon)

Learn what a personal narrative is (hook video) + study the touchstone; choose ONE small moment.

Wk 1 · L2 (Wed)

Plan on the organizer; write the beginning (orientation: when/who/where).

Wk 2 · L3 (Mon)

Write the middle — events in order, past tense, with detail.

Wk 2 · L4 (Wed)

Write the ending. Stretch: add a line of dialogue → full draft.

Wk 3 · L5 (Mon)

Revise with the checklist + peer “2 stars and a wish.”

Wk 3 · L6 (Wed)

Edit, finish (buffer) and publish; celebrate.

The lessons

Lesson 1 · Week 1 · Mon · Aug 31

Purpose: oral rehearsal and small-moment choice

Begin with an oral-only warm-up to build confidence and reinforce sequencing. Display the transition words First, Then, Next, After That, and Finally on a sentence strip. Model a short summer story using the transition words, then invite 1–3 students to share orally. As students speak, gently recast present-tense phrases such as “we go” into past tense: “I/we went.”

Lesson sequence: Watch the short BrainPOP Jr video “Writing About Yourself,” then read the touchstone text Jabari Jumps. Help students notice how the story focuses on one “small moment.” Co-build the rubric, rehearse ideas orally with transition words, and have each student choose one small moment to develop.

Grammar link: Nouns — identify the people and places in the story ideas.

Resources: BrainPOP Jr: Writing About Yourself (Padlet · review-videos) · Jabari Jumps (Padlet · Wk 2 column)

TWR moves: Sentence-expansion warm-up on the touchstone. Kernel: “Jabari jumped.” Ask When? Where? How did he feel? Expand orally together, then every student writes ONE expanded sentence. Low: copy the class sentence and change one word. Med: complete the frame “___ jumped ___ because ___.” High: expand a new kernel alone.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Find the complete sentences (G1) · High: Is it a complete sentence or a fragment? (G3) · Stretch: Complete sentence or fragment? (G4) · Complete sentence, fragment, or run-on? (G5)

★ Product: Partners share small-moment ideas. Each student selects one small moment and gives an oral retell using transition words.

Lesson 2 · Week 1 · Wed · Sep 2

Purpose: write the orientation/beginning — Teach students how to begin a personal narrative by naming when, who, where, and what. Grow the class model by adding a clear beginning.

Lesson sequence: Model how to complete the Scaffolded Outline or folded organizer, reminding students that the organizer will become their draft. Students then write the orientation for their own small moment, using transition words for support.

Resources: Personal Narrative Outline (Scaffolded) (Padlet · Wk 1 column) · Personal Narratives unit doc, p.22 · Word bank: transition words.

TWR moves: Plan in key words first. Before writing the orientation, students dot-jot who / when / where in 2–3 words each (NOT sentences), rehearse the full sentence orally from the jots, then write. Warm-up: finish “Jabari stood on the diving board because… / but… / so…” — say it, then write one.

★ Product: Students complete the orientation—the beginning of their personal narrative.

Differentiation / optional mentor-text support: Use mentor text The Field by Baptiste Paul (read-aloud) if students need another model. Low/Med: draw the narrative beginning, middle, and end first, then share orally before writing. High: fold A3 into eighths—title, orientation, first, then, next, after that, finally, and I felt—and write one sentence per section.

Lesson 3 · Week 2 · Mon · Sep 7

Purpose: build the middle of the narrative. Teach students to tell events in order, write in the past tense, and add 1–2 “show, don’t tell” details. Grow the class model by developing its middle.

Lesson sequence: Oral rehearsal first: students retell their small moment with transition words. Model feeling and five-senses details from a personal story, then students write the middle on the organizer (Hamburger template for support).

Grammar link: Verbs in the past tense + adjectives for description.

Differentiation / extension: High: teacher models a shared-experience narrative. Students begin with one sentence per section, then expand each section to three sentences.

Resources: Hamburger Paragraph template (Padlet · Wk 3 column) · BrainPOP Jr: Writing a Paragraph (Padlet · Wk 3 column) · Word banks: feeling words and five-senses words.

TWR moves: Dot-jot the middle. Students jot their 3 events as key words on the organizer (e.g., “climbed ladder”), say each full sentence aloud from the jot, then write. Upgrade one flat sentence: take “It was fun.” and expand with when? where? why?

Mentor text — Roller Coaster by Marla Frazee (Padlet · Wk 2 column) — quick revisit before writing: how does she stretch ONE small moment into events in order?

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Use time-order words (G2) · High: Form and use the regular past tense (G3) · Extra: Add descriptive details to sentences (G3) · Stretch: Form and use the irregular past tense (G4) · Form and use the irregular past tense (G5)

★ Product: Students draft the middle of their personal narrative, with events in order and at least 1–2 describing details.

Lesson 4 · Week 2 · Wed · Sep 9

Purpose: write the ending and complete the rough draft — Teach students to close their personal narrative by explaining how the moment ended and how they felt. Grow the class model by adding a clear ending.

Lesson sequence: Review the beginning and middle of the class model, then model an ending that answers two questions: How did it end? and How did I feel? Students write their own endings and reread their organizer or draft to make sure it has a beginning, middle, and end.

Stretch option: Students who are ready may add one line of dialogue after watching the Powtoons dialogue video. Dialogue is a “4 — above and beyond” choice, not a requirement.

Resources: Powtoons Dialogue video — stretch (Padlet · Wk 5 column) · Personal Narratives unit doc

TWR moves: Because/but/so for the ending. Students finish all three stems about their own moment: “I felt ___ because… / I wanted to stop, but… / I was brave, so…” The best one becomes (or feeds) the ending sentence. Use the Unit 1 stem bank in the Resource Pack.

★ Product: Students complete a full rough draft with a beginning, middle, and ending.

Lesson 5 · Week 3 · Mon · Sep 14

Purpose: revise for flow and give peer feedback. Teach students to improve a draft, not just finish it; model revising the class model with the checklist before they revise their own.

Lesson sequence: Have students reread their draft aloud ("say it before you fix it") to hear where it bumps. Model a quick self-check against the co-built rubric, then run a partner "2 stars and a wish." Students revise for flow, adding or smoothing transition words so the beginning, middle, and ending connect.

Grammar link: Adverbs — how / when / where the action happened.

Resources: Writing Process Checklist + Paragraph Rules (Padlet · Wk 3 column) · Word bank: transition words.

TWR moves: Sentence combining as the revision move. Model combining two choppy class-model sentences (“I went to the pool. I was scared.” → “I was scared when I went to the pool.”). Each student combines ONE pair in their own draft and varies one sentence opener (don’t start every sentence with “I”).

Mentor text — A Different Pond by Bao Phi (Padlet · Wk 2 column) — read one page and notice the tiny details; students hunt for one place in their own draft to add a detail like that.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Choose the right end mark (G1) · High: Combine sentences by adding key details (G3) · Stretch: Create compound sentences (G4) · Create varied sentences based on models (G5)

★ Product: A revised draft, improved for flow and ready to edit and publish on Wednesday

Lesson 6 · Week 3 · Wed · Sep 16

Purpose: edit, publish, and celebrate the final narrative. This is the exit task — students fix conventions, make a clean copy, and share their small moment with an audience.

Lesson sequence: Model a quick final edit against the co-built rubric: past tense, capital letters, full stops, and best-try spelling. Students edit, then publish — write or type a neat copy with an optional illustration. Finish with an author's-chair share: each writer reads their small moment aloud while classmates give warm "2 stars" feedback, then celebrate the published pieces.

Differentiation / extension: Early finishers begin a second small-moment story.

Resources: Personal Narratives unit doc (Padlet · Wk 1 column) · Co-built rubric for the final check.

TWR moves: Listener summary sentence. After each author’s-chair read-aloud, listeners say (Low/Med) or write (High) one summary sentence: “___’s story was about the time ___.” Writing about a classmate’s story is the comprehension check.

★ Product: EXIT TASK — a published, illustrated Personal Narrative ("My Small Moment"), read aloud to the class.

The lessons

Lesson 1 · Week 1 · Mon · Sep 21

Purpose: introduce procedural writing and why precise steps matter. Launch with the Exact Instructions Challenge and name the parts of a procedure.

Lesson sequence: Students dictate how to make cereal while you follow literally, drawing out why clear, ordered steps matter. Together name the parts of a procedure — Title/Goal, Materials, Steps, Conclusion — and co-build the rubric. Explain the Science-Fair goal: students will write the how-to for their own experiment.

Grammar link: Command (imperative) verbs — the action word that starts each step.

Resources: Exact Instructions Challenge (Padlet) · BrainPOP Jr: How-To Essay (Padlet)

TWR moves: Sentence or fragment? Sort step cards from the cereal challenge (“Pour the milk.” vs “the milk” — which one can a robot follow?). Students fix two fragments by adding a command verb. Builds the complete-sentence habit the whole how-to depends on.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Identify commands (G1) · High: Statement, question, command, or exclamation? (G3) · Stretch: Declarative, interrogative, imperative, or exclamatory? (G4) · Declarative, interrogative, imperative, or exclamatory? (G5)

★ Product: A class list of the parts of a procedure and a co-built rubric.

Lesson 2 · Week 1 · Wed · Sep 23

Purpose: build the class model with a shared cereal how-to. Use the touchstone read-aloud to reinforce procedure structure.

Lesson sequence: Read How to Wash a Woolly Mammoth and notice its ordered steps. Co-write a short cereal how-to with a clear Title, Materials list, and 3–4 numbered steps. “Say it before you write it”: students chant each step aloud as you scribe, stressing command verbs and logical order so a reader could follow it.

Resources: How to Wash a Woolly Mammoth (Padlet) · Exact Instructions Challenge (Padlet)

TWR moves: Expand a step. Kernel: “Pour the milk.” Ask How much? Where? → “Slowly pour half a cup of milk into the bowl.” Students expand one step of the class cereal model with a how/where answer before it goes in.

★ Product: A shared “how to make cereal” model with Title, Materials, and numbered steps.

Lesson 3 · Week 2 · Mon · Sep 28

Purpose: teach command verbs and time connectives. Show how they keep steps in the right order; grow the class model by adding them.

Lesson sequence: Review the cereal model. Mini-lesson on imperative command verbs (cut, mix, pour) and time connectives (First, Next, Then, After that, Finally). Add them to the class model together, then build a shared word bank students can pull from in their own writing.

Grammar link: Verbs — the imperative/command form.

Resources: BrainPOP Jr: Verbs (Padlet) · How to Trap a Dragon — mentor (Padlet) · Word banks: command verbs, time connectives.

TWR moves: Unscramble the step. Give scrambled word cards for two steps (milk / pour / the / bowl / into); students rebuild each as a complete command sentence with the time connective at the front. Transitions are taught AS a sentence activity, not a poster.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Identify time-order words (G2) · High: Use time-order words (G2) · Extra: Identify action verbs (G3) · Stretch: Identify time-order words (G4) · Past, present, or future tense? (G5)

★ Product: A class word bank of command verbs and time connectives, added to the model.

Lesson 4 · Week 2 · Wed · Sep 30

Purpose: write a how-to from a shared demonstration, using command verbs and time connectives.

Lesson sequence: Do a simple mug-recipe demo (eggs, pizza, or brownie — class votes the week before so ingredients can come from home). Students rehearse the steps orally, then write a clear how-to on the organizer with numbered steps, command verbs, and time connectives.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: sentence-frame organizer (“First, ___.”). High: add a Materials list with amounts and a tip.

Resources: Procedural Texts PPT (Padlet) · Word banks: command verbs, time connectives.

TWR moves: Key-word plan before writing. Students dot-jot the recipe steps in 2–3 words each (“crack egg”, “stir mix”), rehearse aloud from the jots, then write full numbered steps. The jot column IS the plan; the sentence column is the writing.

★ Product: A mug-recipe how-to with numbered steps, command verbs, and time connectives.

Lesson 5 · Week 3 · Mon · Oct 5

Purpose: apply procedure skills to a familiar, student-chosen game.

Lesson sequence: Model turning something you know how to do into clear steps. Students pick a game, rehearse the rules aloud with a partner, then write “How to Play a Game” in numbered, logical order. Add pictures and labels so a reader who doesn’t know the game could play it.

Grammar link: Capitalization — the game’s title and the first word of each step.

Resources: Game how-to template (Padlet) · Word banks: command verbs, time connectives.

TWR moves: “So that” purpose sentences. Model: “Shuffle the cards so that no one knows the order.” Students add ONE ‘so that’ to a step of their game how-to — it forces them to explain why a step matters.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Put the sentences in order (G2) · High: Determine the order of events in informational texts (G3) · Stretch: Put the sentences in order (G4) · Order sentences (G5)

★ Product: A game how-to with clear numbered steps, pictures, and labels.

Lesson 6 · Week 3 · Wed · Oct 7

Purpose: strengthen a how-to by testing it on a reader.

Lesson sequence: Students finish a how-to (their game or a second mug recipe), then swap with a partner who tries to follow the steps exactly. Writers mark any step that was missing or unclear and fix it, reinforcing command verbs and time connectives.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: partner reads the steps aloud together. High: rewrite one vague step to be more precise.

Resources: Procedural Texts PPT (Padlet) · Word banks: command verbs, time connectives.

TWR moves: Expansion as repair. When the partner test finds a broken step, the fix is a question word: Which one? How? Where? Students expand the failed step with the missing answer instead of rewriting everything.

★ Product: A how-to revised after a partner “follow my steps” test.

Lesson 7 · Week 4 · Mon · Oct 12

Purpose: start the Science-Fair procedure by choosing the experiment and planning it.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Choose the right end mark (G1) · High: Order the words to create a sentence (G3) · Stretch: Order the words to create a sentence (G4) · Order words to create a sentence (G5)

★ Product: A planning organizer with the experiment’s Materials and step list.

Lesson 8 · Week 4 · Wed · Oct 14

Purpose: draft the Method section of the experiment procedure.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: order a pre-written set of steps, then copy. High: add a “watch out” tip to a tricky step.

Resources: Co-built rubric for a mid-point self-check · Word banks: command verbs, time connectives.

★ Product: An experiment Method draft with numbered steps, command verbs, and time connectives.

Lesson 9 · Week 5 · Mon · Oct 19

Purpose: build out the procedure with a clear Title/Goal and a complete Materials list.

Lesson 10 · Week 5 · Wed · Oct 21

Purpose: finish the draft with a Conclusion and edit for clarity.

Lesson sequence: Model a Conclusion that tells the reader what to notice or remember, plus a pro-tip and a safety note. Students add theirs, then edit against the rubric — capitals, full stops, command verbs, and step order — and finish any labels or illustrations.

Grammar link: End marks and capitals (editing focus).

Resources: How to Wash a Woolly Mammoth — model ending (Padlet) · Co-built rubric.

The lessons

Lesson 1 · Week 1 · Mon · Nov 16

Purpose: opinion vs. fact — an opinion needs a reason. Teach that an opinion is a belief (not provable like a fact) and needs a reason with "because." Start the class model with a shared opinion + reason.

Lesson sequence: Warm-up: 2-min "Debatable Idea" — opinion + reason on "How can we prepare for natural disasters?" Then read the touchstone The Day the Crayons Quit and sort its statements into fact vs. opinion. Model "I think… because…" with "Do we have a responsibility to help people in need?" Co-build the rubric; students orally rehearse their own opinion + reason with a partner.

Grammar link: Present-tense verbs — "I think," "I believe" as opinion starters.

Resources: The Day the Crayons Quit — mentor text · BrainPOP Jr: Facts and Opinions · Debatable Ideas (Padlet · General Resources)

TWR moves: Make the Debatable-Idea warm-up WRITTEN because/but/so. “We should prepare for natural disasters because… / but… / so…” — rehearse orally, then every student writes one of the three (Low/Med choose ‘because’; High write all three). This one move turns every warm-up into sentence-level practice for the rest of the unit.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Complete the fact and opinion sentences (G2) · High: Distinguish facts from opinions (G3) · Stretch: Distinguish facts from opinions (G4) · Identify an author's statement of opinion (G5)

★ Product: Students orally rehearse and choose one opinion + one reason to write about.

Lesson 2 · Week 1 · Wed · Nov 18

Purpose: write opinion sentences using the OREO frame. Grow the class model by writing 3–4 full sentences: Opinion, Reason, Example, Opinion again.

Lesson sequence: Warm-up: today's Debatable Idea — "How might we reduce, reuse, and recycle?" Review the class model's opinion + reason, then model adding an Example and restating the Opinion (OREO). Students pick a topic — recycling, favorite snack, best recess game — and write their own OREO sentences, checking off each letter.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: sentence frames for each OREO letter ("I think ___ because ___."). High: add a second reason before restating the opinion.

Resources: OREO Opinion Graphic Organizer · Opinion Sentence Starters poster (Padlet · General Resources) · Word bank: because, also, another reason

TWR moves: OREO becomes a key-word outline. The Opinion is written as a full sentence, but R and E go down as dot jots (2–3 words). Students rehearse the full sentences orally from the jots before writing. Ideas first, sentences second.

★ Product: Opinion sentences (OREO) on a low-stakes topic.

Lesson 3 · Week 2 · Mon · Nov 23

Purpose: strong reasons — give THREE reasons for one opinion. Teach that strong opinion writing needs several different reasons, not one idea repeated. Grow the model by adding two more reasons to last week’s opinion.

Lesson sequence: Warm-up: Debatable Idea — "Are video games bad for kids?" Revisit the Needs & Wants worksheet. Model brainstorming three different reasons for an opinion ("Should everyone have the same things?") using the reason word bank. Students orally rehearse their three reasons with a partner, then list them on the organizer.

Grammar link: Adjectives — precise describing words make a reason more convincing.

Resources: Needs & Wants worksheet (Padlet · Wk 1 column) · IXL: Choose Reasons to Support an Opinion · Word bank: because, also, another reason

TWR moves: Three DIFFERENT because-sentences. One ‘because’ sentence per reason; then the distinctness check — partners read all three and ask: are these three different reasons, or one reason said three ways?

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Complete the opinion passage with a reason (G2) · High: Choose reasons to support an opinion (G3) · Stretch: Choose reasons to support an opinion (G4) · Choose reasons to support an opinion (G5)

★ Product: Three reasons listed for one opinion (needs vs. wants).

Lesson 4 · Week 2 · Wed · Nov 25

Purpose: write a full opinion paragraph — statement + 3 reasons + conclusion. Grow the class model into a complete paragraph, not just sentences.

Lesson sequence: Warm-up: Debatable Idea — "What is climate change, and should we act on it?" Model turning three reasons into a paragraph: opinion statement, three reasons in order, closing sentence. Students write their own opinion paragraph on needs vs. wants, using sentence starters ("In my opinion… First… Also… Another reason…").

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: paragraph frame with a sentence starter for each reason. High: add a linking word (however, in addition) between reasons.

Resources: Needs & Wants worksheet (Padlet · Wk 1 column)

TWR moves: Write the paragraph FROM the outline. Topic sentence (opinion) → one sentence per reason jot → concluding sentence. Students tick each jot as it becomes a sentence, so nothing is skipped and nothing is copied.

Mentor text — I Wanna Iguana by Karen Kaufman Orloff (YouTube read-aloud) — Alex piles up reason after reason in his letters; tally his reasons and check each one has a “because.” Watch: read-aloud video

★ Product: Opinion paragraph — statement + 3 reasons + conclusion.

Lesson 5 · Week 3 · Mon · Nov 30

Purpose: back up a reason with evidence. Teach that a strong reason is backed by a fact, not just a feeling. Grow the model by adding one piece of evidence to a reason.

Lesson sequence: Warm-up: Debatable Idea — "Are self-driving taxis a good or bad idea?" Recall the trade/Global Goods learning (imports, local goods, the Lulu Express visit). Model turning a reason into evidence using "For example…" and "I learned that…". Students add a fact from the trade learning to one reason in their paragraph.

Grammar link: Conjunctions — "because," "since," and "for example" link a reason to its evidence.

Resources: Imports & Exports worksheet (Padlet · Wk 2 column) · trade PowerPoint (Padlet · Wk 2 column) · Word bank: for example, I learned that, since

TWR moves: Expand a reason with evidence. Take one reason sentence and expand it with For example… / I learned that… using a fact from the trade unit. Writing the fact is the check that they learned it.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Complete the opinion passage with an example (G2) · High: Complete the opinion-reason-example table (G3) · Stretch: Remove the sentence that does not belong (G4) · Combine main ideas from two texts (G5)

★ Product: One reason backed by a fact or example.

Lesson 6 · Week 3 · Wed · Dec 2

Purpose: revise the whole paragraph to add evidence throughout.

Lesson sequence: Warm-up: Debatable Idea — "Should schools ban mobile phones?" Reread the paragraph aloud ("say it before you fix it"). Model adding "For example…" or "I learned that…" to a second reason in the class model. Students revise their own paragraph to back up as many reasons as they can with evidence.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: add evidence to just one reason, using a sentence-starter bank. High: add evidence to all three reasons.

Resources: Imports & Exports worksheet (Padlet · Wk 2 column)

TWR moves: Combine reason + evidence. Model joining a reason and its example into one sentence with ‘because’ or ‘since’ (“Local food is fresher since it does not travel far.”). Students combine one pair in their own paragraph.

★ Product: Evidence-backed opinion paragraph.

Lesson 7 · Week 4 · Mon · Dec 7

Purpose: introduce the counterargument. Teach that a persuasive writer names the other side’s view and answers it — "Some people think… but I believe… because…" Grow the model by adding a counterargument sentence.

Lesson sequence: Warm-up: Debatable Idea — "Should single-use plastics be banned?" — a great two-sided topic. Model the frame "Some people think… but I believe… because…" using a real other-side view on local vs. imported goods. Students orally rehearse a counterargument for their own opinion with a partner, then add it to their draft.

Grammar link: Conjunctions (however, but) — signal the shift to the opposing view.

Resources: Writing to Persuade: Counterargument · Word bank: some people think, but I believe, however

TWR moves: BUT is the counterargument machine. The full frame is one sentence: “Some people think ___, but I believe ___ because ___.” Rehearse orally with a partner on their own topic, then write it. One taught conjunction does the whole rhetorical job.

Mentor text — Hey, Little Ant by Phillip & Hannah Hoose (YouTube read-aloud) — the boy and the ant argue BOTH sides; T-chart their reasons: the perfect model for “Some people think…, but…” Watch: read-aloud video

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Use conjunctions (G2) · High: Use coordinating conjunctions (G3) · Stretch: Use subordinating conjunctions (G4) · Use coordinating conjunctions (G5)

★ Product: One counterargument sentence added to the draft.

Lesson 8 · Week 4 · Wed · Dec 9

Purpose: add a strong conclusion to complete the persuasive draft.

Lesson sequence: Warm-up: Debatable Idea — "World population has reached 8 billion — good or bad?" Model a conclusion that restates the opinion in new words and leaves the reader thinking (not just "The End"). Students write their conclusion, then reread the whole piece to confirm it has all its parts.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: conclusion sentence starter ("In conclusion, I believe ___ because ___."). High: end with a question or call to action for the reader.

Resources: Co-built rubric (self-check for all parts)

TWR moves: SO for the conclusion. Stem: “So, we should ___.” Students draft the conclusion from a ‘so’ sentence, then rewrite it with a different opener (“That is why…” / “In conclusion…”) so it doesn’t echo the warm-up.

★ Product: Full persuasive draft (opinion, 3 reasons, evidence, counterargument, conclusion).

Lesson 9 · Week 5 · Mon · Dec 14

Purpose: revise for persuasive language and clarity.

Lesson sequence: Warm-up: Debatable Idea — "Should scientists try to bring back extinct animals?" Reread the draft aloud. Model checking the co-built rubric and strengthening word choice (replacing "good" with a stronger persuasive word). Run a partner "2 stars and a wish," then revise for stronger reasons and clearer evidence.

Grammar link: Linking words — because, also, however, in addition — vary the reason connectors.

Resources: Peer Editing Checklist: Opinion Writing

TWR moves: Revision-moves menu (pick two): expand one thin sentence with when? why?; combine two choppy sentences; change one sentence opener. Name the move as you model it — students revise with moves, not vibes.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Use linking words to complete a passage (G2) · High: Choose the best transition (G3) · Stretch: Choose the best transition (G4) · Choose the best transition (G5)

★ Product: A revised draft, strengthened and ready to edit and publish.

Lesson 10 · Week 5 · Wed · Dec 16

Purpose: edit, publish, and present the persuasive piece — the exit task.

Lesson sequence: Warm-up: Debatable Idea — "Could there be life on an earth-like planet?" Model a final edit against the rubric: capitals, end marks, best-try spelling. Students edit, then publish a neat copy with an optional illustration. Finish with a share: each writer presents while classmates give warm "2 stars" feedback.

Differentiation / extension: Early finishers begin a second opinion piece on a new topic.

Resources: Co-built rubric (final check)

TWR moves: Listener summary sentence: after each presentation, the audience writes “___ believes ___ because ___.” Collect as the unit’s listening-comprehension exit slip.

★ Product: EXIT TASK — a published persuasive piece, presented to the class.

The lessons

Lesson 1 · Week 1 · Mon · Jan 4

Purpose: what is research? Choose a focused topic. Teach that research means finding answers to real questions using reliable sources, not just copying. Start the class model by choosing a shared class topic.

Lesson sequence: Watch a short "What is Research?" clip and discuss why researchers check reliable sources. Model narrowing a broad idea ("Qatar") into a focused topic using a mind map. Students brainstorm and choose their own focused topic, then help sort example sources into reliable vs. unreliable. Co-build the rubric.

Grammar link: Nouns — proper nouns for the people, places, and things in your topic.

Resources: Reliable Sources · What is Research? for Kids

TWR moves: Because/but/so in content. Warm-up: “Qatar changed quickly because… / but… / so…” — say, then write one. The mind map stays key-words-only (it already is a TWR-style plan — keep it that way).

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Identify questions (G1) · High: Statement, question, command, or exclamation? (G3) · Stretch: Order items from most general to most specific (G4) · Read about history (G5)

★ Product: A focused topic and a mind map of subtopics.

Lesson 2 · Week 1 · Wed · Jan 6

Purpose: write three research questions.

Lesson sequence: Model turning a topic into a real question ("What…", "Why…", "How…"), not a yes/no question. Students write THREE research questions about their topic, then trade with a partner to check that each one can’t be answered in a single word.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: question stems ("What is ___?", "Why does ___?", "How do ___?"). High: rank the questions from most to least important.

Resources: How to Choose a Research Topic

TWR moves: Question-word expansion. Give topic key words; students turn them into real questions with who/what/when/where/why/how stems. Low: match question-word cards to half-built questions, then copy the best one. Med: complete question stems (“Why does ___?”). High: write three and test each by the one-word rule (if a partner can answer in one word, expand the question).

★ Product: Topic + 3 research questions.

Lesson 3 · Week 2 · Mon · Jan 11

Purpose: search skills — find two reliable sources. Teach keyword searching and kid-safe search engines so students can find real answers to their research questions.

Lesson sequence: Model turning a research question into search keywords (cutting small words like "the," "is"). Demonstrate a kid-safe search engine (Ducksters, Nat Geo Kids). Students search for and bookmark TWO sources for their topic, checking each against last week’s reliable-vs-unreliable chart.

Grammar link: Question words (who, what, where, when, why, how) — turn a question into search keywords.

Resources: How to Search · Nat Geo Kids · Ducksters

TWR moves: Summary sentence as the product check: “My best source is ___ because ___.” One written sentence proves both the search skill and the reliability thinking.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Determine the topic and purpose of informational passages (G2) · High: Determine the main idea of a passage (G3) · Stretch: Identify primary and secondary sources (G4) · Determine the main idea of a passage (G5)

★ Product: Two bookmarked, reliable sources for the topic.

Lesson 4 · Week 2 · Wed · Jan 13

Purpose: take notes in your own key words — not copying.

Lesson sequence: Model reading a short passage, then jotting 3–4 key words instead of copying full sentences. Students read source 1 and take notes in their own key words on the graphic organizer, checking that no sentence is copied word-for-word.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: fact frame ("One fact is… According to my source…"). High: note at least five facts, grouped by subtopic.

Resources: What is Note-Taking?

TWR moves: Key-word notes are already the TWR move — protect them. Add the reverse check: students pick two jots and say each back as a full sentence to a partner. If they can’t, the note was copied, not understood.

★ Product: Notes from source 1, in the student’s own words.

Lesson 5 · Week 3 · Mon · Jan 18

Purpose: take notes from a second source.

Lesson sequence: Review what makes notes "your own words" rather than copying. Students read source 2 (or an Epic Books nonfiction text on their topic) and take notes in key words, adding to their organizer from last week.

Grammar link: Commas — separate items in a list of facts.

Resources: What is Note-Taking? · Epic Books: Middle East Collection · BrainPOP Jr: Commas with Adjectives and Lists

TWR moves: Comma-in-a-list sentence from jots. Students take three related jots and write one sentence with a comma list (“Qatari divers collected pearls, oysters, and shells.”). Grammar practiced by writing in content, not spotting.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Select the detail that does not support the topic sentence (G2) · High: Organize information by topic (G3) · Stretch: Organize information by topic (G4) · Read graphic organizers (G5)

★ Product: Notes from source 2, in the student’s own words.

Lesson 6 · Week 3 · Wed · Jan 20

Purpose: organize notes into two categories.

Lesson sequence: Model sorting a mixed set of notes into two clear categories (e.g., "Then / Now" or "History / Culture"). Students sort their own notes from both sources into two categories on a graphic organizer, ready to become body paragraphs.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: teacher pre-labels the two categories. High: add a third category if the topic needs it.

Resources: Organizing Research Notes

TWR moves: Name this the report OUTLINE (a TWR Transition Outline): two category headings + key-word details under each. Rule stays key-words-only. Students rehearse one category aloud as sentences before Monday’s drafting.

★ Product: Organized notes, sorted into two categories.

Lesson 7 · Week 4 · Mon · Jan 25

Purpose: write a hook introduction. Teach that a strong introduction hooks the reader and names the topic. Grow the class model by drafting an introduction.

Lesson sequence: Watch the Strong Introductions video and discuss what makes a hook work. Model a hook using the starter "The country of ___ has a very ___ history/culture." Students write their own hook introduction, naming their topic.

Grammar link: Adjectives — vivid, specific describing words make a hook stronger.

Resources: Strong Introductions

Low: choose one modelled hook and copy-adapt it to their topic (swap the underlined words). Med: complete one hook frame (“Did you know that ___?”). High: try two different hook types and pick the better one.

Lesson 8 · Week 4 · Wed · Jan 27

Purpose: write body section 1 from your notes.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: paragraph frame with one sentence per fact. High: add a transition sentence into body 2.

Resources: Co-built rubric (mid-point self-check)

TWR moves: Draft FROM the outline: each jot becomes one full sentence; tick jots as they are used. Combine two related facts with ‘and’ or ‘but’ (“Then pearls were valuable, but now oil is.”).

★ Product: Introduction + body section 1.

Lesson 9 · Week 5 · Mon · Feb 1

Purpose: write body section 2 and the conclusion.

Lesson sequence: Model turning Category 2 notes into body section 2, then model a conclusion that sums up what the reader should remember. Students write their body section 2 and conclusion, naming their source(s).

Grammar link: Colons — introduce a list of facts or examples.

Resources: Conclusion

★ Product: Body section 2 + conclusion.

Lesson 10 · Week 5 · Wed · Feb 3

Purpose: add a Table of Contents and a glossary.

Lesson sequence: Watch the Table of Contents and Glossary videos. Model building a contents page (section names + page numbers) and a glossary of 3 key terms from the report. Students add their own Table of Contents and glossary, plus one "then vs. now" sentence if their topic allows it.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: glossary sentence frame ("___ means ___."). High: add a fourth glossary term.

Resources: Table of Contents · Glossary

TWR moves: Appositives define glossary words. Model: “A dhow, a wooden sailing boat, carried the divers.” Say the pattern as a chant: name — comma — what it is — comma — rest of the sentence. Low: match term cards to definition cards, then copy the built sentence. Med: complete the frame “A ___, a ___, ___.” with a word bank. High: write one appositive sentence per glossary term.

Mentor text — A Nat Geo Kids article on a Qatar-adjacent topic — look at how a real information page uses headings, bold words, and a glossary before adding yours.

Lesson 11 · Week 6 · Mon · Feb 8

Purpose: revise and polish grammar.

Lesson sequence: Reread the draft aloud against the co-built rubric. Model fixing a run-on with a comma or semicolon and checking possessive apostrophes. Students revise their own report for clarity, then run a partner check using the rubric.

Grammar link: Possessive apostrophes and semicolons — clear, precise sentences.

Resources: BrainPOP Jr: Possessive Nouns

TWR moves: Revision with the complete-sentence test — no grammar terms needed. Read each sentence aloud and ask two questions: WHO or WHAT is it about? WHAT HAPPENED? If either answer is missing, fix it together. Then expand one thin fact sentence with when? where? why? (Low: teacher marks the sentence to fix; High: also split one too-long sentence into two.)

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Form the singular possessive (G1) · High: Identify and correct errors with plural and possessive nouns (G3) · Stretch: Identify and correct errors with plural and possessive nouns (G4) · Form the singular or plural possessive (G5)

★ Product: A revised, polished report ready to publish.

Lesson 12 · Week 6 · Wed · Feb 10

Purpose: publish and present the research report — the exit task.

Lesson sequence: Model a final proofread against the rubric. Students publish a neat copy (with Table of Contents, glossary, and any diagrams), then present their report to the class or a partner, naming their sources.

Differentiation / extension: Early finishers add a labeled diagram or map to their report.

Resources: Co-built rubric (final check)

TWR moves: Open every presentation with the one-sentence summary of the whole report, then present the sections. Low/Med read it from a sentence strip; High speak it from key words.

Scaffolds for ELL / SPED: nature word banks (sight/sound/touch words); a syllable-counting routine (clap/tap) for haiku; rhyme word lists (RhymeZone); poem frames (line starters); thesaurus for stronger words; tiered support (fill-in poem frame / word bank / open).

TWR 2.0 upgrade: the quick review is now a 3–5 min WRITTEN sentence activity in unit content (expand a kernel sentence, because/but/so, or combine two short sentences); planning happens in key-word dot jots (2–3 words, never full sentences) so ideas come before sentence-building; and every Wednesday closes with one summary sentence. Every move runs on the same ladder for low-language learners: SAY it → COPY the class sentence → COMPLETE a frame → CREATE your own; Low always has a frame and a word bank. Each lesson below carries a “TWR moves” line; templates and per-unit stem banks are in the TWR 2.0 Resource Pack.

Unit exit task

Student-friendly task: Make a small set of nature poems — a sense poem, a short rhyming poem, and a haiku (5-7-5) — using strong describing words and clear pictures in the reader's mind. Choose your best, illustrate it, and read it aloud.

How the lessons build to it:

Wk 1

Read poems; build a nature word bank; write a sense poem.

Wk 2

Rhyme patterns (AABB); write a rhyming poem.

Wk 3

Haiku (5-7-5); write a haiku.

Wk 4

Revise word choice, illustrate, publish & read aloud.

The lessons

Lesson 1 · Week 1 · Mon · Feb 15

Purpose: what is poetry? Notice imagery. Teach that poems paint word-pictures using the five senses, and that poets choose words on purpose. Start the class model with a shared nature word bank.

Lesson sequence: Read aloud Shel Silverstein’s "Sick" and notice its rhythm and fun word choices. Watch a short clip from the Writing Poetry for Kids series. Co-build the rubric, then build a class nature word bank (sight, sound, smell, touch words) for a shared topic (e.g., the beach or the desert).

Grammar link: Adjectives — precise sense words paint a clearer picture.

Resources: Writing Poetry for Kids — Ep.1: What is Poetry? (Padlet · Topic 3: Poetry AABB ABAB) · BrainPOP Jr: Poems · "Sick" by Shel Silverstein — read aloud

TWR moves: Sentence expansion IS the imagery engine. Kernel: “The waves crash.” Ask Where? When? How? → “At night, the grey waves crash against the rocks.” Expanded sentences get broken into poem lines later — word banks feed the expansions.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Choose the sensory details that match the picture (G2) · High: Identify sensory details (G3) · Stretch: Identify sensory details (G4) · Sort sensory details (G5)

★ Product: A class nature word bank (sight, sound, smell, touch words).

Lesson 2 · Week 1 · Wed · Feb 17

Purpose: write a sense (list) poem — no rhyme needed.

Lesson sequence: Model turning word-bank words into short poem lines, one sense per line. Students pick a nature topic and write their own sense poem, checking off each sense they used.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: sentence frame ("I see ___. I hear ___."). High: add a line that compares the topic to something else ("like…").

Resources: Senses Poem Writing Template · Word bank: sight, sound, smell, touch words

TWR moves: Jot one key word per sense before writing (see: ___, hear: ___…). Then each jot grows into a line. Low/Med use the frame; High expand each line with a where or when.

★ Product: Sense poem.

Lesson 3 · Week 2 · Mon · Feb 22

Purpose: rhyme patterns — AABB and ABAB. Teach that rhyming poems follow a pattern, and that end words in the same line-pair usually rhyme. Grow the model by rhyming a nature line.

Lesson sequence: Read a few rhyming nature poems and clap out the AABB pattern (lines 1–2 rhyme, 3–4 rhyme). Model looking up a rhyme on RhymeZone for a nature word from the class bank. Students build a short rhyme word list for their own nature topic.

Grammar link: Nouns — rhyme words are often nouns naming things in nature.

Resources: Writing Poetry for Kids — Ep.6: What is Rhyming? (Padlet · Topic 3: Poetry AABB ABAB) · Example Poems — AABB/ABAB rhyme schemes (Padlet · Topic 3: Poetry AABB ABAB) · RhymeZone (Padlet · Topic 2: Dictionary and Thesaurus Skills)

TWR moves: Scrambled-line activity: word cards for one line, rebuilt so the rhyme word lands at the end (“the sun goes down / all over town”). Syntax practice disguised as a puzzle.

Mentor text — Messy Room by Shel Silverstein (Padlet · mentor poems) — read it aloud and clap the rhyme pattern before building your own rhyme list.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Complete the rhyme (G1) · High: Label the rhyme scheme (G3) · Extra: Complete the poem with a word that rhymes (G2) · Stretch: Label the rhyme scheme (G4) · Label the rhyme scheme (G5)

★ Product: A rhyme word list for a chosen nature topic.

Lesson 4 · Week 2 · Wed · Feb 24

Purpose: write a short rhyming poem (AABB) about nature.

Lesson sequence: Model writing two rhyming couplets using the rhyme list. Students write their own AABB poem, checking the pattern by reading it aloud and clapping the rhymes.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: sentence frames with the rhyme word already placed at the end of each line. High: try an ABAB pattern instead.

Resources: RhymeZone (Padlet · Topic 2: Dictionary and Thesaurus Skills)

TWR moves: Combine two short lines where the rhythm bumps. Model: “The bird sings. It sits in the tree.” → “The bird sings high up in the tree.” Students try one combine in their draft.

★ Product: Rhyming poem (AABB).

Lesson 5 · Week 3 · Mon · Mar 1

Purpose: haiku — 3 lines, 5-7-5 syllables. Teach the haiku form and a clap/tap routine for counting syllables. Grow the model with one class haiku about nature.

Lesson sequence: Watch the BrainPOP Jr Poems video’s haiku section and read example haiku. Model clapping the syllables in a line, then co-build one class haiku about a shared nature topic, checking 5-7-5 together.

Grammar link: Nouns and adjectives — the fewest, best words for a tight 5-7-5 line.

Resources: Haiku Poetry for Kids (Padlet · Week 4: Haiku Poems) · BrainPOP Jr: Poems

TWR moves: Haiku is a key-word form — say so. Students jot 3 nature key words, clap the syllables of each jot FIRST, then build lines around the jots.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: How many syllables does the word have? (G1) · High: Sort by the number of syllables (G2) · Stretch: Order adjectives (G4) · Choose the synonym (G5)

★ Product: One class haiku, syllables checked.

Lesson 6 · Week 3 · Wed · Mar 3

Purpose: write one or two haiku about nature.

Lesson sequence: Students pick a nature theme and draft their own haiku, clapping each line to check the syllable count before writing it down. Partners check each other’s syllable counts.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: word bank of pre-counted nature words (sun-set = 2, oc-ean = 2). High: write a second haiku on a different nature theme.

Resources: Writing a Haiku Poem worksheet (Padlet · Week 4: Haiku Poems) · Ocean Haiku Poem template with word bank (Padlet · Week 4: Haiku Poems)

TWR moves: Word-swap within the syllable budget: trade a weak word for a precise one with the same count (“big” → “vast”, “nice” → “bright”). Thesaurus + clap check together.

★ Product: Haiku (one or two), syllables checked.

Lesson 7 · Week 4 · Mon · Mar 8

Purpose: revise word choice for stronger imagery.

Lesson sequence: Model looking up a plain word (e.g., "big," "nice") in a thesaurus and swapping in a stronger, more precise word. Students choose their best 1–2 poems and revise at least two words using a thesaurus, then add an illustration.

Grammar link: Adjectives and verbs — precise words replace vague ones.

Resources: BrainPOP: Dictionary and Thesaurus (Padlet · Topic 2: Dictionary and Thesaurus Skills) · Thesaurus.com

TWR moves: Writer’s because-sentence: after revising, students write “I chose the word ___ because ___.” One sentence of writing ABOUT writing — the reflection is itself sentence practice.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Choose the synonym (G2) · High: Revise the sentence using a stronger verb (G3) · Extra: Find synonyms in context (G3) · Stretch: Revise the sentence using a stronger verb (G4) · Find synonyms in context (G5)

★ Product: Revised, illustrated poem(s).

Lesson 8 · Week 4 · Wed · Mar 10

Purpose: publish and read a poem aloud — the exit task.

Lesson sequence: Model reading a poem with expression (pace, pauses, emphasis). Students assemble their best poems into a small anthology or poster, then read one poem aloud to the class with expression.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: practice the read-aloud with a partner first. High: memorize a short poem to perform without reading.

Resources: Poetry Anthology Project

TWR moves: Listener summary line after each read-aloud: “___’s poem paints a picture of ___.” Say it (Low/Med) or write it (High) on a strip for the poet to keep.

★ Product: EXIT TASK — published nature-poetry anthology, read aloud to the class.

Grade 5 Writing — Unit 6: Fictional Narrative

6 weeks · Mar 21 – Apr 29 · Exit task: “My Story” (any topic) — a published imaginative story presented at the exhibition · 2026–2027 · Mon & Wed writing lessons

Unit exit task

Student-friendly task: Write and publish a one-page imaginative story about ANY topic you choose — space, adventure, animals, magic, sport, anything with a clear beginning, middle and end, a character who faces and solves a problem, and — as a stretch — a line of dialogue and a piece of figurative language. Then read it aloud at the exhibition.

How the lessons build to it:

Wk 1

Genre + story-map; invent a character & setting.

Wk 2

Problem & goal; plan the events.

Wk 3

Write the beginning + middle (show, don't tell).

Wk 4

Write the ending; stretch: dialogue + figurative language.

Wk 5

Revise with checklist & peer feedback; edit.

Wk 6

Publish and present at the exhibition.

The lessons

Lesson 1 · Week 1 · Mon · Mar 22

Purpose: what makes an imaginative narrative? Teach the story-map elements (character, setting, problem, events, solution) using the touchstone text. Start the class model by naming its parts.

Lesson sequence: Watch "What is an Imaginative Narrative" (Episode 1) and read/watch the touchstone How to Catch a Star. Together, label the story-map with the touchstone’s character, setting, problem, and solution. Co-build the rubric.

Grammar link: Nouns — the character (who) and setting (where) are both nouns.

Resources: What is an Imaginative Narrative (Padlet · How to Write an Imaginative Narrative series) · How to Catch a Star — read aloud (Padlet · mentor read-alouds)

TWR moves: But/so IS story grammar. Retell the touchstone as one sentence set: “The boy wanted a star, but it was too far away, so he ___.” Co-write it under the story-map — every story the class meets this unit gets the same one-sentence treatment.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Choose the picture that matches the setting or character (G1) · High: Identify story elements (G3) · Stretch: Identify story elements (G4) · Identify story elements (G5)

★ Product: A labeled story-map for the touchstone text.

Lesson 2 · Week 1 · Wed · Mar 24

Purpose: invent a character and a setting.

Lesson sequence: Model inventing a character (name, one trait) and a setting (any world they like — space, jungle, city, under the sea…) for the class story on the story-map. Students invent their own character and setting, then orally rehearse describing both to a partner.

Grammar link: Adjectives — describing words that make a character or setting vivid.

Resources: Create a Character worksheet (Padlet · student organizers) · Brainstorming Settings worksheet (Padlet · student organizers)

TWR moves: Appositive introduces the character: “Zara, a brave astronaut, stepped onto the red sand.” Model it, then students write their character’s first sentence with an appositive (Low/Med frame: “___, a ___ ___, …”). Expansion for setting: Where? When?

Mentor text — The Snowy Day by Ezra Jack Keats (Padlet · mentor read-alouds) — how do the words make the setting feel real? Steal one setting move.

★ Product: Character + setting on the story-map.

Lesson 3 · Week 2 · Mon · Mar 29

Purpose: story problem and goal. Teach that every story needs a problem the character wants solved. Grow the model by adding a problem to the class story.

Lesson sequence: Watch a short problem-and-solution story-elements clip and discuss what "problem" and "goal" mean in a story. Model adding a problem and a goal to the class story-map. Students add a problem + goal for their own character to their story-map, then orally rehearse it with a partner.

Grammar link: Verbs — "wants," "needs," "tries" show a character’s goal.

Resources: Story Elements: Problem & Solution (video) · Planning Out the Problem worksheet (Padlet · student organizers) · Goals & Challenges worksheet (Padlet · student organizers)

TWR moves: The problem is one BUT sentence: “___ wanted ___, but ___.” Rehearse orally, write it on the story-map. If the ‘but’ sentence works, the story works.

Mentor text — Enemy Pie (Padlet · mentor read-alouds) — name the problem and the goal, then say them together as one “but” sentence.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Identify the problem and solution in a story (G1) · High: Order events in a story (G2) · Stretch: Make predictions about a story (G4) · Summarize a story (G5)

★ Product: Story problem + goal on the story-map.

Lesson 4 · Week 2 · Wed · Mar 31

Purpose: plan the events — beginning, middle, end.

Lesson sequence: Model planning the class story’s events in three parts on the story-map: beginning, middle, end. Students plan their own events in the same three parts, then orally rehearse telling the whole story to a partner before writing.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: event sentence frames for each part. High: add a small setback in the middle before the problem is solved.

Resources: Story Events List organizer (Padlet · student organizers) · Co-built rubric (self-check for story-map completeness)

TWR moves: Events as dot jots only (2–3 words per event) across beginning–middle–end. Oral rehearsal of the whole story FROM the jots — the retell is the draft-before-the-draft.

Mentor text — A Bad Case of Stripes (Padlet · mentor read-alouds) — list its beginning–middle–end events on the board as dot jots.

★ Product: Story plan (problem + events, beginning–middle–end).

Lesson 5 · Week 3 · Mon · Apr 5

Purpose: write the beginning. Teach that a strong beginning introduces the character in the setting, then the problem. Grow the class model by drafting its beginning.

Lesson sequence: Model writing the class story’s beginning from the story-map. Students write their own beginning: character + setting + problem, using their story-map as the plan.

Grammar link: Past-tense verbs — narratives are told in the past tense.

Resources: How to Catch a Star — read aloud (Padlet · mentor read-alouds)

TWR moves: Write from the jots: each beginning jot becomes a sentence; expand the opening sentence with where and when. The appositive character-introduction from Week 1 leads it off.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Add descriptive details to sentences (G2) · High: Show character emotions and traits (G3) · Stretch: Identify the narrative point of view (G4) · Create varied sentences based on models (G5)

★ Product: Story beginning (character, setting, problem).

Lesson 6 · Week 3 · Wed · Apr 7

Purpose: write the middle — "Show, Don’t Tell."

Lesson sequence: Model turning a flat sentence ("She was scared.") into a Show-Don’t-Tell sentence ("Her hands shook as she stared at the door.") using feeling and sense words. Students write the middle of their story — the events in order — using at least one Show-Don’t-Tell sentence.

Differentiation / extension: Low/Med: feeling-word bank and a sentence frame ("___ felt ___ because ___."). High: add two Show-Don’t-Tell sentences.

Resources: Show, Don’t Tell · “Show, Don’t Tell” 16-chart pack (Padlet · “Show, Don’t Tell” charts) · Word bank: feeling words, five-senses words

TWR moves: Show-don’t-tell = expansion with How? Take the flat sentence and interrogate it: “She was scared.” — How do we know? What did her hands do? → “Her hands shook as she stared at the door.” Students expand one flat sentence in their middle.

★ Product: Beginning + middle.

Lesson 7 · Week 4 · Mon · Apr 12

Purpose: write the ending.

Lesson sequence: Model an ending that solves the problem and tells how the character feels. Students write their own ending, then reread the whole story to check it has a beginning, middle, and end.

Grammar link: Past-tense verbs (review) — check the ending stays consistent with the rest of the story.

Resources: Co-built rubric (self-check for a complete draft)

TWR moves: SO ends the story: “So ___ finally ___.” plus a because-feeling: “She smiled because ___.” Two frames give a resolution AND a feeling — the two things the rubric asks of an ending.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Insert dialogue into a story (G2) · High: Use actions and dialogue to understand characters (G3) · Stretch: Use actions and dialogue to understand characters (G4) · Punctuating dialogue (G5)

★ Product: Full draft (beginning, middle, ending).

Lesson 8 · Week 4 · Wed · Apr 14

Purpose: stretch — add dialogue and figurative language.

Lesson sequence: Watch the dialogue mentor clip and model punctuating one line of dialogue. Model one simile or metaphor in the class story. Students who are ready add one line of dialogue and/or one simile or metaphor to their draft.

Stretch option: Dialogue and figurative language are a "4 — above and beyond" choice, not a requirement.

Resources: Dialogue mentor · Story Dialogue Planning worksheet (Padlet · student organizers) · Quotation Marks for Direct Speech · BrainPOP Jr: Similes

TWR moves: Stretch combine: join an action and a line of dialogue with ‘as’ or ‘while’ (“‘Hold on!’ she shouted as the rocket shook.”). Only for students taking the dialogue stretch.

★ Product: Full draft (+ stretch: dialogue, figurative language).

Lesson 9 · Week 5 · Mon · Apr 19

Purpose: revise with the checklist and peer feedback.

Lesson sequence: Reread the draft aloud. Model checking the co-built rubric and running a partner "2 stars and a wish." Students revise their story-map order, add missing details, and strengthen weak sentences.

Grammar link: Adjectives and feeling words — replace plain words with stronger ones.

Resources: Story Elements Checklist (Padlet · student organizers) · Co-built rubric (peer-feedback self-check)

TWR moves: Revision-moves menu (pick two): combine two choppy sentences; vary two sentence openers (not every sentence starts with the character’s name); expand one thin sentence with how? or why?

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Revise the sentence using a stronger verb (G2) · High: Combine sentences by adding key details (G3) · Stretch: Revise the sentence using a stronger verb (G4) · Create compound sentences (G5)

★ Product: A revised draft, strengthened and ready to edit.

Lesson 10 · Week 5 · Wed · Apr 21

Purpose: edit and strengthen word choice.

Lesson sequence: Model a final edit against the rubric: past tense, capitals, full stops, best-try spelling. Students edit their story, then swap one plain word for a stronger one in two places.

Differentiation / extension: Early finishers add a title and a labeled illustration.

Resources: Co-built rubric (final check)

TWR moves: Complete-sentence test on your own story — no grammar terms. Read each sentence aloud: WHO is it about? WHAT HAPPENED? Fix any sentence missing an answer (teacher plants one broken sentence in the class model to find first). Word-swap (two plain words → stronger words) stays.

★ Product: Edited, revised story ready to publish.

Lesson 11 · Week 6 · Mon · Apr 26

Purpose: publish the story.

Lesson sequence: Students make a neat, illustrated final copy (handwritten or typed) of their story, adding a title and cover illustration. This is a buffer lesson for finishing touches before the exhibition.

Differentiation / extension: Early finishers rehearse their read-aloud with a partner.

Resources: Co-built rubric (final check)

TWR moves: The back-cover blurb is a summary sentence: “This story is about ___, who wanted ___, but ___.” Published copies carry it on the back — a real audience for a taught summary frame.

IXL practice (Gr 1–3): Low: Order images in a story (G1) · High: Put the sentences in order (G3) · Stretch: Summarize a story (G4) · Order sentences (G5)

★ Product: A published, illustrated story on the student’s chosen topic.

Lesson 12 · Week 6 · Wed · Apr 28

Purpose: present the story — the exit task.

Lesson sequence: Model reading a short excerpt with expression. Students present their story at the exhibition (read-aloud to classmates, a partner class, or visitors), then reflect on one thing they’re proud of.

Resources: Co-built rubric (final check)

TWR moves: Listener summary sentence after each exhibition read-aloud: “In ___’s story, ___ solved the problem by ___.” Collected strips become the celebration wall.

★ Product: EXIT TASK — a published imaginative story, presented at the exhibition.
