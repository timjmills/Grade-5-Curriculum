Guided Reading — Character Traits. Materials: short story or excerpt (from a class book or a provided short text); chart or board.

Introduction (3 min): define character traits as qualities that describe what a character is like on the inside (e.g., brave, selfish, kind). Ask students to describe someone from a favorite book or movie using a few traits.

Guided practice (7 min): read a short passage featuring a main character; list the character's traits on the board and ask students for evidence from the text (what the character says, does, or thinks).

Independent practice (5 min): students pick one character and list two traits with a supporting example from the text in their notebooks.

Closing (1 min): recap — character traits describe who a character is based on thoughts, actions, and words.

Guided Reading — Character Motivation. Materials: same story/excerpt as Sunday.

Introduction (3 min): motivation is what pushes a character to act — what a character wants or needs. Example: if a character steals food, their motivation might be hunger.

Modeling (5 min): look at a scene from the story and ask, "Why did the character do this?" If a character helped someone, their motivation might be kindness or wanting to make a new friend.

Guided practice (5 min): read another short passage; students identify the character's action and discuss why the character acted that way, supported with evidence from the text.

Closing (2 min): understanding a character's motivation helps us understand their actions and the story.

Rotations: see the station list from Sunday or the differentiation row below.

Phonics: SeeSaw Controlled R

Bossy Finger Game — read a word list aloud, some with bossy "r" and some without; students point a bossy finger when the "r" is bossy. Words: jar, art, farm, store, corn, orange, dirt, stir, girl, turn, fur, hurt, germ, her.

Guided Reading — Character Relationships. Materials: same story/excerpt as previous days.

Introduction (3 min): characters have relationships with other characters (friends, family, enemies) that help us understand them better.

Modeling (5 min): pick two characters and explain their relationship and how it affects the story's events (e.g., "These two are friends and help each other — this shows they are loyal").

Guided practice (5 min): students think of how two characters interact and how their relationship affects the plot; share ideas with the class.

Closing (2 min): understanding relationships helps us see how characters grow and how they affect the plot.

Rotations: see Sunday's station list or the differentiation row below.

Guided Reading — Character Development & Theme. Materials: same story/excerpt as previous days.

Introduction (3 min): as a story progresses, characters can change or learn something new — character development. The character's growth often connects to the theme (message) of the story.

Modeling (5 min): discuss how the character changed from beginning to end. Example: "At first the character was afraid of the dark, but by the end they were brave — the message might be about overcoming fear."

Guided practice (5 min): students think about how the main character changed and what they learned; guide them to connect this change to the theme or message.

Closing (2 min): character development is important because it helps convey the story's message or theme.

Rotations: see Sunday's station list or the differentiation row below.

Review / catch-up — revisit last week's Explorer learning; finish unfinished investigations or SeeSaw posts; preview this week's topic.

Mon · Explore + Choose: Pick Your Science Fair Question

1. Hook — Show a finished example project (last year's egg-float investigation) on the Science Fair Presentation Template so students see what the finished product looks like.

2. Browse ideas — In pairs, browse the Ideas for Science Fair handout (12 tested project ideas: slime, dissolving eggshells, Mentos & soda, pipe-cleaner circuits, rusting steel wool, invisible ink, Alka-Seltzer, chalk & vinegar, penny oxidation, baking powder vs. soda, milk-and-vinegar plastic, tie-dye) or propose an original idea connected to states of matter or changes in matter.

3. Choose — Each student picks a question and states it in “How does ___ affect ___?” form.

4. Start planning — Begin Step 1 of the Project Planning Worksheet: Developing Your Question.

5. Teacher check — Teacher approves each question before students move on.

Objective: Can state a testable science-fair question connected to states of matter or changes in matter.

Assessment: State the research question in “How does ___ affect ___?” form.

Differentiation: Choose from 2 teacher-picked options with support / choose from the full list independently / design an original question independently.

Tue · Plan Your Fair Test: Variables & Hypothesis

1. Review — Revisit yesterday's chosen questions; recall the fair-test rule (change one thing, keep the rest the same).

2. Teach — Identify the independent variable (what you change) and dependent variables (what you measure — aim for at least 3 things to observe, e.g. colour, time, size).

3. Complete — Step 2 of the Project Planning Worksheet: Variables and Measurement (independent variable, dependent variables, constants).

4. Write — Draft a hypothesis in IF…THEN…BECAUSE form (Step 3 of the worksheet).

5. Share — Pairs check each other's hypotheses: is it testable? Does it explain why?

Objective: Can identify an independent variable and at least 3 dependent variables, and write a testable hypothesis.

Assessment: Name the independent variable and 3 things you will measure; state a hypothesis using the sentence frame.

Differentiation: Identify variables with a sentence-starter bank / with some support / independently.

Wed · Finalize Your Plan: Materials, Procedure & Safety

1. Review — Revisit variables and hypothesis from yesterday.

2. Complete — Steps 4–6 of the Project Planning Worksheet: materials needed, safety considerations, data-collection plan, and timeline.

3. Write — Draft the numbered procedure step-by-step, using the Science Fair Presentation Template's Procedure slide as a model — enough detail that someone else could repeat it.

4. Teacher sign-off — Teacher approves the finished plan.

5. Send home — Send home the Science Fair Prep Letter and the Parent Note (Tri-Board) so families know what to gather and bring in.

Objective: Can list the materials, safety steps, and a clear numbered procedure for their investigation.

Assessment: Procedure is written in clear, numbered, repeatable steps.

Differentiation: Use a procedure sentence frame with support / with some support / write independently.

Resources — New Materials – Science Fair Prep Letter · New Materials – Parent Note (Tri-Board)

Review / catch-up — consolidate Mon–Wed learning; complete recordings/reflections; reteach or extend as needed.
